# FlowMinute Lab — Growth & Editorial Production Standard (v2)

**Status: ADOPTED** | **Effective: 2026-10-09** | **Owner: FlowMinute Lab**  
Applies to all future YouTube long-form videos, Shorts, videos made from trends, and automation-supported production.

## Mission and success definition
Build an original, credible, entertaining tech-education channel. Our job is to deliver **valuable information that viewers actively choose to watch**, not merely to produce uploads.

North star: growing **satisfied, returning viewers and subscribers**, supported by impression-to-view conversion, meaningful watch time, audience retention, shares, and substantive comments. No promised views, virality, or revenue.

## Audience and editorial boundaries
Cover AI, machine learning, practical data science, Excel/Power Query, automation, AI agents, real-world workflow problems, useful software releases, and noteworthy technology. Make content accessible to students, office workers, creators, and beginner/intermediate tech audiences.

Each item must have a real human relevance question: **What happened? What can someone do? What is surprisingly difficult or risky? What was tested?**

## Production target (quality overrides quota)
- **Daily target when instructed to produce the day's package:** **2 original Shorts + 1 original long-form video.**
- **Extra coverage:** 1+ additional Shorts or a separate timely video **only** when a verified high-relevance news event deserves a distinct and timely angle.
- Never upload redundant variations, padded scripts, or low-quality clips to meet a number. If minimum quality cannot be reached, explicitly report the shortfall and publish fewer rather than sacrificing originality.
- This document specifies production rules; **it does not create an unattended daily task**. Research and release occur when instructed or when a separate scheduled automation has actually been created.
- Default time candidates: **11:00 AM, 4:00 PM, and 8:30 PM Asia/Kolkata**, tested rather than asserted to be optimal. Publish important verified breaking news the same day if safely ready; exact time depends on the announcement and channel data.

## Mandatory fresh topic research at each requested production session
1. Verify the target publishing date/time zone and inspect the current channel's content calendar to avoid duplicates.
2. Scan **today and the previous day** for meaningful releases and updates; look back 3–7 days only when useful for context.
3. Start with **official product announcements, release notes, changelogs, vendor documentation**, then credible reporting and community discussions; cross-check what is accessible vs. previewed vs. announced.
4. Check YouTube Studio **Trends** (breakouts, audience interests, content gaps), recent channel Analytics, and similar-channel videos. Do not claim exact search volume without evidence.
5. Maintain at least **three possible topic candidates**; record supporting URLs, dates, relevant user problem, and what is actually new.
6. Score each candidate (0–5 for each) on **freshness; audience fit; demonstrability; useful payoff; curiosity; evidence quality; original angle; effort versus available deadline**.
7. Choose one balanced long-form topic and two distinct Shorts. Avoid making Shorts interchangeable recaps of the long video; each should work independently.
8. Skip the trend when there is no verified substance, little audience fit, or insufficient time to make something genuinely good. Use the high-quality evergreen reserve.

### Topic mix — working hypothesis to test, NOT measured optimum
- ~50% useful tutorials, real problems, and evergreen solutions.
- ~30% timely releases and clearly explained capabilities.
- ~20% original experiments, comparisons, mistakes, myths, and case studies.
This mix is a testable starting point, not a fixed quota.

### Breaking-news gate for extra video
Add an extra video only if **all** are true: (a) confirmed via primary sources, (b) genuinely relevant to FlowMinute viewers, (c) a *different* compelling question/benefit than the scheduled content, (d) original visuals and fact-checking can be completed in time, and (e) it does not disrupt already verified schedules. Record why the additional upload was justified.

## Core creative rule: informative AND interesting
We should earn attention every few moments, not put facts on a static slide and hope the viewer stays.

**Every script starts with a one-line creative promise**: “After watching, the viewer will understand/see __ and be able to __.”

Design **hook + real example/proof + payoff** before drafting the whole narration. Show the interesting or surprising element early, accurately matching title and thumbnail. Use curiosity without withholding the answer deceptively.

**Rotate formats** rather than reusing one stock structure:
- **Problem → failure → investigation → fix**
- **Live experiment / documented benchmark → result → limitation**
- **Big tech release → what changed → practical demo → catch**
- **A-vs-B comparison on identical tasks → honest winner / trade-offs**
- **Misconception → reveal with evidence → useful rule**
- **Build a simple agent or automation → visualize decisions → verify outcome**

### Shorts
- Opening ~0–2 seconds: specific, truthful hook with immediately understandable imagery. No long intro or brand animation.
- Remaining sequence: a visible example, stakes, essential explanation, early payoff, meaningful question. Length serves the idea: do not pad to 40 seconds.
- Mobile-first typography, strong contrast, safe subtitle zones, limited simultaneous elements, strong audio clarity.
- Avoid premature “like and subscribe” requests or artificial cliffhangers. Looping is optional, not a substitute for delivering the answer.

### Long-form
- Hook establishes what is at stake; first 30 seconds must honor the title and thumbnail.
- Keep a narrative progression, practical evidence, useful nuance, and a satisfying payoff.
- Include clear explanation of limitations and trade-offs; put the strongest demonstration early enough to be seen.
- Duration is determined by content quality and evidence, not arbitrary quota.

## Visual production rules (choose deliberately)
Use visual forms as appropriate, not all in every video:
1. **Actual screen recording or genuine screenshots** for testable app behavior; accurate data and current UI.
2. **Cinematic, photorealistic scenes** to make the human problem and stakes relatable, with appropriate rights and disclosure.
3. **Clean UI callouts and step-by-step animations** for the exact interaction being explained.
4. **Agent/AI architecture or process diagrams** to explain data flow, permissions, tools, memory, failures, and human review—only when explanatory.
5. **Comparison tables, timelines, charts, annotated before/after** for meaningful results.
6. **Sound design, music, transitions, camera movement** only when they support clarity or emotion; no visual noise.

**Every visual must do one job:** create curiosity, provide evidence, explain a relationship, or deliver the payoff. Reject decoration that competes with narration. All text and elements must be legible on a phone. Avoid repeated generic office footage, static faux app screens, or same-looking sequences across different episodes.

**Claims and synthetic media:** Never imply a staged mockup is a real product interface, a proposed workflow actually ran, a person endorsed the content, or a benchmark was conducted if it wasn't. Mark reenactments/concept demos clearly. Follow YouTube's altered/synthetic-content disclosure rules for realistic content when applicable. Use properly licensed original imagery, video, music, and narration. Cite or link factual claims and record feature access and rollout limitations.

## Hard gates before publishing
A video may only be **READY_FOR_UPLOAD** after all applicable tests pass:

- [ ] Current topic research verified, source dates and links captured.
- [ ] Title/thumbnail accurate, intriguing, not misleading; no unsupported “I tested” claim.
- [ ] Distinct angle and example; no mass-produced near-duplicate content.
- [ ] Storyboard with visual proof and payoff; no boring intro or repeated filler.
- [ ] Technical facts, units, calculations, examples, and product access limits checked.
- [ ] Real footage versus fictional mockups clearly distinguished where relevant.
- [ ] Thumbnail legible on phone; clean 16:9 and 9:16 safe zones; no clipping or excessive empty space.
- [ ] Voiceover pacing, music mix, sound clarity, captions, timing, and scenes reviewed.
- [ ] Final MP4 quality (resolution, codec, no silent ending, duration, visual contact sheet and sample full-playback inspection) checked.
- [ ] Thumbnail, metadata, links, content disclosures, required audience designation, and captions ready or their absence explicitly documented.
- [ ] Existing YouTube Studio schedule inspected to avoid duplicate uploads.

A rendered MP4, successful GitHub Action, or prepared metadata **never** means that YouTube publishing is complete.

## Upload, verification and receipts
Upload as private; verify title, description, custom thumbnail where supported, disclosure, audience designation, captions, and Studio checks. Schedule to the correct **Asia/Kolkata** date/time. Inspect Studio's explicit successful scheduling confirmation and video ID. Write a per-item `state/scheduled/<id>.json` receipt (ID, full URL, local date/time, QA result, source and exception notes). Never silently re-upload a scheduled video; update existing metadata instead.

## Analytics and scientific learning
For each published video, record at **24h, 48h, and 7d** when analytics are available. YouTube retention can lag 1–2 days. Segregate Shorts and long-form; compare like with like and avoid conclusions from tiny samples.

**Shorts:** shown in feed; chose to view vs swipe; engaged views; average view duration/percentage viewed; likes, shares, meaningful comments, subscriber gains; opening drop-offs.

**Long-form:** impressions; thumbnail CTR; 30-second intro retention; average view duration; audience retention peaks/dips; returning viewers; subscribers; shares/comments; traffic source.

**Audit questions for the next release:**
- Did the packaging attract the *right* audience, not just clicks?
- Which frame, explanation, or demonstration caused a retention dip or spike?
- Did viewers stay to the promised payoff?
- Did the Short drive qualified viewers to a related long video?
- Are viewers returning and asking follow-up questions?
- What **one creative variable** will we deliberately change next?

Treat titles/thumbnails, hooks, content categories, and times as hypotheses. Use valid A/B tests only when the tool and sample allow. Don't call results “viral” just because early views rose.
