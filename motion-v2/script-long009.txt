Imagine opening Chrome and telling it, "Help me order the groceries for this week." Then imagine your browser visiting sites, comparing options, and preparing the task while you're doing something else. Sounds useful. But before you hit Start, there is one screen I would never skip.
This week, Google expanded Gemini in Chrome to Android users in India. There's an important distinction here: an assistant that helps you understand a page is not the same thing as an agent that clicks around the web for you.
Let's make it concrete. First, the regular Gemini in Chrome experience. You're reading a long article on your phone. Instead of scrolling through twenty paragraphs, you ask, "What's the main point?" Or you ask a question about the page. That's about reading and understanding, not taking over your browser.
Now the paid part. Google says auto browse can handle multi-step web tasks, including finding groceries, filling forms, or making reservations. In India, this part requires an eligible Google AI Pro or AI Ultra subscription. It is rolling out, so paying alone does not guarantee that every device has the feature today.
Here's an illustrative example, not a real purchase or a recording of Google's product. I ask the browser to find ingredients for a simple dinner, keep the total under twelve hundred rupees, and leave the final choice to me. Watch how much more complicated that request is than a simple search.
First, the assistant has to understand the task. Then it chooses which sites to visit. Then it searches, compares, and prepares an action. If a site needs personal details or an account, that's no longer just an answer on a screen. Real websites and real information are involved.
So the most important moment is not when the AI finds the items. It's when you review the proposed plan before it starts. Google tells users to check the page context, preferences and personal information that might be shared with sites. For sensitive actions, auto browse is designed to seek confirmation, but users still need to pay attention.
Here's my three-second checklist before I let an AI browser act. One: are these the sites I intended? Two: will it share an address, phone number, or other private information I didn't mean to provide? Three: is there a clear review point before a purchase or another important action?
If the answer feels wrong, don't start the task. You can take over instead. An automated browser can make mistakes. A confident-looking plan is not proof that the final result is correct.
There's also an access catch. The Android rollout initially supports English, and Google's own help says it may not show up for everyone immediately. On supported Android devices, you need recent Chrome, a signed-in account, and the appropriate setup. Auto browse additionally requires eligible paid access and permission to browse on your behalf.
So here is my verdict. Gemini in Chrome makes more sense when it helps you understand information quickly. Auto browse becomes interesting when it saves real steps, but only if you can see and control what it does.
