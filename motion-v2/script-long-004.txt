This spreadsheet looks finished. Five invoices, a tidy total, and a formula with no error message. But the total is wrong by three thousand six hundred dollars. Not because Excel cannot add. The formula simply missed the final row. That is the problem with automatically suggested formulas: they can look perfect and still answer the wrong question. Today I will show three practical checks for Copilot formula suggestions, with realistic but fictional examples, and the safe way to use the feature.

First, the range check. Here is a five-row invoice table. The amounts are four thousand two hundred, three thousand eight hundred, five thousand seven hundred, five thousand two hundred, and three thousand six hundred. A formula that sums only the first four invoices gives eighteen thousand nine hundred. The correct five-row total is twenty-two thousand five hundred. The formula is valid. No red error appears. We catch it by following the blue range highlight and counting the records. If a suggestion appears, inspect where it starts and ends. Verify the input range, then compare the result against an independent total. In a real workbook, use an Excel Table where appropriate, because structured references are often easier to maintain when data grows. But still inspect the logic.

Second, the meaning check. Imagine a cost of one hundred dollars and a selling price of one hundred fifty. Ask for profit margin, and someone might divide fifty by one hundred, giving fifty percent. That is markup on cost. Margin divides profit by selling price, giving thirty-three point three percent. Both calculations are valid formulas. Only one matches the question. AI cannot rescue an ambiguous instruction unless you clarify the business definition. Write the calculation in plain language. Profit margin equals selling price minus cost, divided by selling price. Then test one row with values you can calculate yourself.

Third, the edge-case check. Excel Copilot can suggest a formula from sample values. You enter Ava for Ava Stone, then Leo for Leo Kim, and a plausible rule takes everything before the first space. That works for two simple names. But what if the data contains Doctor Maya Chen? The naive rule returns Doctor, not Maya. That does not prove any specific Copilot suggestion is wrong. It shows why a good example can still be an incomplete specification. Test titles, blank cells, extra spaces, and special names before you accept a formula for the entire column. If the logic breaks, improve the examples or choose an explicit rule suited to the data.

Now, what actually exists in Excel? Microsoft says Copilot formula completion can offer a full formula when you type equals, using nearby headers, cells, and tables. A suggestion card can explain what it does and preview the result. Formula by Example can detect patterns from values you enter and offer a reusable formula instead of pasted text. Availability depends on the Microsoft 365 and Copilot experience you have. This video uses illustrative worksheet views, not a claim that these exact suggestions were generated live.

And there is a useful control. In Excel for Windows, you can go to File, Options, then Copilot to turn off suggestions for completing formulas or suggestions based on patterns. In Excel for the web, open File, Options, then Copilot Settings. Microsoft says these preferences persist until you turn them back on.

My checklist is simple. One: does the range include every record? Two: does the formula compute the meaning you requested? Three: does it survive messy real-world data?
